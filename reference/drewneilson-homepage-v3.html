Smash*It · Business / Experience
01 / The wrong product

I thought I was selling destruction.

People paid us to smash things. That was the transaction. But it wasn't the whole experience—and understanding the difference changed how I thought about the business.

Look inside this story +
The discovery

Customers weren’t all using the room the same way. Some savored the full session; others smashed everything in minutes. The experience, rather than the inventory of objects, became the interesting design problem.

A related question

What are people really buying when they appear to be buying an activity?

Explore connections ↑
02 / THE WRONG METRIC

What if more people winning means a worse game?

An escape room with a 90% success rate was telling us less than we thought. Adjusting the challenge changed the experience—not just the number.

Explore the decision +
The discovery

A 90% escape rate sounded encouraging. But it suggested that success alone wasn’t capturing the experience. After reworking the rooms, escape rates eventually settled around 70–75%.

A related question

Is the obvious measure actually measuring the thing that matters?

Explore connections ↑
03 / THE WRONG FORMAT

A city tour isn't really about the city.

A moving stage, a peculiar vehicle, and the logistical work of making a tour feel spontaneous. A different way of thinking about what a guest came for.

Explore the work +
Behind the experience

Routes, scripts, guide training, and live adaptation all had to work together. What felt spontaneous to a guest required deliberate coordination behind the scenes.

A related question

What invisible systems make an experience feel effortless?

A tiny thought experiment

Would you rather understand all the rules of an experience before entering it—or discover them as you go?

Why ask?
Because the answer changes the experience itself. The disclosure of rules is part of the design. It's also a question worth asking about ordinary systems, not just games.
